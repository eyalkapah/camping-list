# The claim is an explicit button, and nobody is publicly named for being behind

Five UI prototypes (`prototypes/ui/a1`–`a5`) put seventeen variants against identical
data. Two of the verdicts are decisions rather than preferences, and are recorded here.

**Claiming an Item is an explicit `אני` button, not a row tap or a swipe.** A row tap is
faster one-handed and was the obvious choice for the most-repeated action in the app. We
rejected it because the failure modes are not symmetric: a slow claim costs a second, while
an accidental claim silently tells ten families that the ketchup is handled, and nobody
discovers the lie until the campsite. The prototype made the mis-tap easy to feel while
scrolling a thirteen-category list.

**The per-family checklist shows your own family in full and everyone else in aggregate.**
The matrix variant was the most informative screen we built, and the nudge button was
trivial to implement. Both were rejected: this is a WhatsApp group of friends, and a
standing public table of who has not confirmed water converts a logistics app into a
social instrument. Chasing stays human — an Announcement written by a person.

## Consequences

Claiming costs one accurate tap rather than one loose one; if people complain about the
friction, the answer is a bigger button, not a bigger hit target on the row.

No screen can answer "who specifically hasn't confirmed?". That information exists in the
data and is deliberately not surfaced. Adding an organiser-only view of it would reopen
this decision, not sidestep it.
